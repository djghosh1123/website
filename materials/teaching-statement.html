Teaching statement

Dhrubajyoti Ghosh

My goal as a statistics instructor is not simply to teach students how to fit a model, but to help them understand why a method is needed, when it is valid, how it is implemented, and what its output means in the context of a scientific or practical question. I therefore organize my teaching around a progression from motivation and statistical reasoning to computation, interpretation, and application.

Teaching through motivation, reasoning, computation, and interpretation

Statistics becomes meaningful when students can see the question before they see the formula. I typically begin a new topic with a concrete problem: What does an odds ratio actually tell us? Why can two forecasting models with similar in-sample fit behave differently out of sample? Why does regression adjustment not automatically make an observational comparison causal? Once the question is clear, I develop the statistical reasoning and assumptions behind the method, connect the theory to computation in R or Python, and return to interpretation.

This approach is especially important in graduate courses, where rigor and accessibility have to coexist. I want students to see enough mathematical structure to understand what a method is doing without allowing derivation to become an end in itself. In Applied Categorical Data Analysis, for example, I connect likelihood-based models, coefficient interpretation, diagnostics, and real-data analysis rather than treating them as separate topics.

Computation is integrated into that intellectual structure rather than treated as a separate laboratory skill. Students use code to simulate data, examine assumptions, compare estimators, visualize uncertainty, and explore what happens when ideal conditions fail. My goal is for students to become confident enough to adapt an analysis to a new problem rather than simply reproduce a template.

Curriculum development and reflective course design

A defining feature of my first three semesters as a faculty member has been curriculum development. I developed two new graduate courses—Time Series Forecasting and Causal Inference—and substantially redesigned Applied Categorical Data Analysis, rebuilding lecture materials, examples, assessments, and computational components around a coherent progression of ideas.

Developing these courses has reinforced my view that course design is itself an iterative, evidence-based process. I use student feedback to refine pace, signposting, guided coding, examples, recaps, and the distinction between foundational ideas and extensions. I regard this cycle—teach, gather evidence, reflect, revise—as an essential part of effective instruction.

I also place considerable value on accessibility outside the classroom. I encourage questions, maintain regular office hours, respond promptly to student emails, and make detailed notes, code, and supporting materials available so that students can revisit difficult ideas.

Mentoring students toward independent scholarship

I view research mentoring as an extension of teaching. As Rabindranath Tagore wrote, “A teacher can never truly teach unless he is still learning himself.” That idea resonates strongly with my approach to mentoring: the most productive student projects are collaborative learning experiences in which I provide structure and technical guidance while gradually transferring ownership of the scientific question to the student.

At Kennesaw State, I have mentored students at the undergraduate, master’s, and doctoral levels and have tried to build a genuine research pipeline rather than a collection of isolated projects. Early in a project, I typically provide a structured reading plan, reproducible code, simulation exercises, and short-term milestones. As students gain confidence, I shift responsibility toward study design, methodological choices, interpretation, writing, and presentation.

My goal is for students to leave the mentoring relationship able to formulate a question, identify appropriate tools, recognize limitations, communicate results clearly, and eventually mentor others themselves.
