  1. † Ibenye, C., † Jimenez, A.-V., † Jaeger, S. & Ghosh, D. When Machines Lie Differently: Detecting AI vs Human Fake News. Accepted at ICMLA (2026).

    Summary. This study asks whether fake news is equally detectable when it is written by humans versus generated by AI. Using the same feature representation and modeling pipeline for both tasks, the paper compares real news with human-written fake news and with AI-generated fake news using lexical-diversity, readability, and emotion features together with logistic regression, random forests, support vector machines, gradient boosting, neural networks, and an ensemble model.

    Across all models, AI-generated fake news was substantially easier to distinguish from real news: most models achieved AUCs above 0.99 for the AI-generated task, compared with roughly 0.89–0.96 for human-written fake news. Feature analyses suggest that AI-generated misinformation currently shows more uniform patterns in readability and emotional expression, whereas human-written fake news more closely resembles legitimate journalism.

    Undergraduate research project: This work was developed with undergraduate researchers Calvin Ibenye, Aya-Vera Jimenez, and Samuel Jaeger through Kennesaw State University’s First-Year Scholars Program.

  2. Ghosh, D. A Rank-Based Information Fusion Framework for Comparing Clustered Multivariate Socioeconomic Outcomes. IEEE International Conference on Fuzzy Systems (FUZZ-IEEE), 1–5 (2026).

    Summary. This paper develops a multivariate, distribution-free rank-based framework for comparing clustered and correlated outcomes across groups. The method adapts the Longitudinal Rank-Sum Test (LRST) to a clustered cross-sectional setting, treating states as independent units and counties as dependent observations within states, while combining several socioeconomic outcomes into a single omnibus comparison.

    The framework is applied to pooled U.S. county data from 2019–2023 to compare states with and without refundable Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC) policies across poverty, income inequality, housing cost burden, medical care costs, and per-capita income. Across repeated county subsampling and different cluster sizes, counties in EITC states consistently showed more favorable joint socioeconomic rankings. The analysis is explicitly descriptive rather than causal and illustrates how rank-based information fusion can provide robust, interpretable comparisons when outcomes are multivariate and clustered.

  3. Ghosh, D., Boettcher, W., Johnston, R. & Lahiri, S. Machine Learning Based Bot Detection on X With Temporal and Semantic Feature Integration. IEEE Transactions on Computational Social Systems (2026).

    Summary. This paper develops an interpretable machine-learning framework for distinguishing organic users from social-media bots using information extracted from users’ X/Twitter histories. Rather than relying only on profile metadata or opaque deep-learning representations, the method combines temporal behavior—such as periodicity, ARIMA-based dynamics, and periodogram structure—with semantic features including lexical diversity, hashtag use, word-frequency patterns, and sentiment.

    Using verified organic and inorganic accounts, the study evaluates k-means clustering and support vector machines and finds that combining temporal and semantic features yields the strongest discrimination. The framework also adds split conformal prediction to quantify uncertainty, producing calibrated prediction sets alongside class labels; the conformal analysis reported approximately 94.7% test accuracy with an AUC of 0.978.

    Interdisciplinary collaboration: Developed with William Boettcher and Rob Johnston at North Carolina State University, combining social-science questions with statistical and machine-learning methodology.

  4. Ghosh, D., McElroy, T. & Lahiri, S. Polyspectral Mean Estimation of General Nonlinear Processes. Bernoulli (2026), in press.

    Summary. This paper develops a general inferential theory for polyspectral means—weighted averages of higher-order spectra designed to capture nonlinear and non-Gaussian dependence that ordinary second-order spectral methods can miss. It establishes asymptotic normality for polyspectral-mean estimators of arbitrary order and derives an explicit limiting variance involving higher-order polyspectra, providing a unified basis for large-sample inference.

    The paper also proposes a new bispectrum-based test of the linear-process hypothesis. Simulations study finite-sample behavior, while applications illustrate the methodology through linearity testing for sunspot data and clustering GDP growth trajectories across countries using bispectral features.

    Invited presentations: This work has also been the subject of multiple invited talks on higher-order spectral analysis and nonlinear time-series inference.

  5. Ghosh, D. & Pal, S. Efficacy Analysis in Clinical Trials: A Comprehensive Review of Statistical and Machine Learning Approaches. The New England Journal of Statistics in Data Science, 1–23 (2026).

    Summary. This review organizes the major methodological families used for efficacy analysis in clinical trials, spanning classical parametric procedures, nonparametric methods, Bayesian approaches, and modern machine-learning techniques. It emphasizes when each framework is most appropriate, the assumptions each requires, and how the choice of method changes with longitudinal, skewed, high-dimensional, or otherwise complex trial data.

    The paper also discusses missing-data mechanisms, adaptive and personalized trial settings, deep learning, reinforcement learning, and emerging computational approaches. Its central goal is to connect traditional statistical inference with newer predictive methodology while making the tradeoffs in robustness, interpretability, uncertainty quantification, and scalability explicit.

  6. Ghosh, D., Tushar, F., Dahal, L., Vancoillie, L., Lafata, K. J., Samei, E., Lo, J. Y. & Luo, S. Demographic distribution matching between real-world and virtual phantom population. Medical Physics 53, e70364 (2026).

    Summary. This paper introduces DISTINCT (Distributional Subsampling for Covariate-Targeted Alignment), a framework for selecting a subsample from a large real-world cohort whose joint demographic distribution matches that of a virtual phantom population. The method aligns continuous and categorical covariates together and evaluates distributional similarity using Wasserstein and Kolmogorov–Smirnov distances.

    Applied to the National Lung Screening Trial and the Virtual Lung Screening Trial, DISTINCT identified an aligned NLST cohort of 9,974 participants. Downstream lung-cancer risk-score performance stabilized once aligned samples exceeded roughly 6,000 participants, illustrating how demographic alignment can support more representative comparisons between real and virtual imaging studies.

    Virtual imaging collaboration: Conducted with Duke’s Center for Virtual Imaging Trials and supported in part by NIH/NIBIB funding.

  12. Ghosh, D., Boettcher, W. A., Johnston, R. & Lahiri, S. THANOS: A Predictive Model of Electoral Campaigns Using Twitter Data and Opinion Polls. Data Science in Science 4, 2484180 (2025).

    Summary. This paper develops two statistical forecasting frameworks that combine traditional opinion polls with social-media information. THOS uses Twitter hashtag frequencies, while THANOS augments those signals with network-structure features such as centrality measures, with the goal of using fast-moving online information alongside slower but more representative polling data.

    The models are evaluated on Ireland’s 2018 referendum on the 36th constitutional amendment and the 2018 U.S. Senate elections. In those studied cases, the paper reports that the simpler THOS model worked well when margins were relatively clear, whereas network information became more useful in closely contested races. The results are presented as an empirical forecasting study rather than as a general guarantee about future elections.

    Interdisciplinary collaboration: Developed with William Boettcher and Rob Johnston in Political Science at North Carolina State University.

  13. Ghosh, D. & Luo, S. A non-parametric U-statistic testing approach for multi-arm clinical trials with multivariate longitudinal data. Journal of Multivariate Analysis 209, 105447 (2025).

    Summary. This paper extends the Longitudinal Rank-Sum Test from two-arm trials to multi-arm randomized clinical trials with multiple longitudinal outcomes. The resulting U-statistic framework provides a global, distribution-free comparison across treatment arms while retaining the temporal and multivariate structure of the data.

    Simulation studies show strong Type I error control and improved power relative to applying separate two-arm LRSTs with multiplicity correction. An application to the Bapineuzumab 301 Alzheimer’s disease trial demonstrates how the method can be used to evaluate several dose groups against a common control and then identify promising treatment arms after a global rejection.

  14. Ghosh, D., Pal, S., Lutz, M., Luo, S. & ADNI. Ensemble survival analysis for preclinical cognitive decline prediction in Alzheimer’s disease using longitudinal biomarkers. Journal of Alzheimer’s Disease 107(3), 1256–1266 (2025).

    Summary. This study develops an ensemble survival framework for predicting progression from cognitively normal status to mild cognitive impairment or Alzheimer’s disease using longitudinal ADNI biomarkers. The approach combines penalized Cox models with Random Survival Forest, DeepSurv, and XGBoost, and then aggregates predictions using ensemble averaging and Bayesian model averaging.

    Among 721 initially cognitively normal participants, the ensemble achieved a peak C-index of 0.907 and integrated time-dependent AUC of 0.904, compared with a C-index of 0.608 for baseline-only models. A notable practical finding is that adding one follow-up visit produced most of the predictive gain, while a second follow-up added relatively little.

  15. Ghosh, D., Xu, X., Luo, S. & CPP Integrated Parkinson’s Database. Power and sample size calculation for multivariate longitudinal trials using the longitudinal rank sum test. Statistics in Medicine 44, e70261 (2025).

    Summary. This paper develops the power and sample-size theory needed to use the Longitudinal Rank-Sum Test prospectively when designing multivariate longitudinal clinical trials. It derives the LRST distribution under both null and alternative hypotheses, obtains closed-form expressions for power and sample size, and develops consistent estimators with large-sample justification.

    Simulation studies validate the calculations, and applications to Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s disease trials illustrate how the framework can be used to plan adequately powered studies when several outcomes are followed repeatedly over time.

  19. Xu, X., Ghosh, D., Luo, S. & CPP Integrated Parkinson’s Database. A novel longitudinal rank-sum test for multiple primary endpoints in clinical trials: Applications to neurodegenerative disorders. Statistics in Biopharmaceutical Research (2025).

    Summary. This paper introduces the Longitudinal Rank-Sum Test (LRST), a nonparametric U-statistic-based global test for clinical trials with multiple primary endpoints measured repeatedly over time. The method aggregates evidence across outcomes and visits into a single omnibus test, avoiding separate multiplicity adjustment while accommodating non-normal and heterogeneous outcome distributions.

    Simulations examine a range of realistic trial settings, including conflicting effects across endpoints, and demonstrate Type I error control together with competitive power. Applications to neurodegenerative-disease trial data illustrate how LRST can summarize treatment efficacy across cognitive, functional, and other longitudinal domains.

    Foundational LRST paper: This article introduces the core LRST framework that underlies the later multi-arm, power/sample-size, missing-data, and covariate-adjusted extensions.

    Summary. This study evaluates photon-counting CT for quantitative lung-density measurement and compares it with conventional energy-integrating CT. It combines retrospective clinical data with scanner-specific virtual imaging experiments, allowing reconstruction parameters and radiation dose to be varied systematically against known virtual ground truth.

    The analysis identifies imaging settings associated with lower lung-density error and reports that, under the optimized settings studied, photon-counting CT achieved lower lung and emphysema density error than the compared energy-integrating CT scanners. The work illustrates how virtual imaging trials can complement clinical data when controlled repeated imaging experiments would be impractical in patients.
